Waste Oil to Wing

Taiwan imports over 96% of its energy and buys nearly all its jet fuel — including the "green" kind — from abroad. Yet it exports a lot of the very oil that could make that fuel at home. This project asks: how much of the gap could Taiwan actually close on its own?

The core finding of this project: Taiwan's recovered UCO is already fully committed — to raw export or biodiesel — leaving essentially zero "spare" oil for domestic SAF. Any real increase requires actively redirecting oil away from what it's already used for, not just collecting more.

Quick glossary

UCO
Used Cooking Oil — the raw waste oil itself. Not a fuel yet.
Biodiesel
A fuel made from UCO for diesel engines — trucks, generators. Different product from SAF.
SAF
Sustainable Aviation Fuel — also made from UCO, but refined further so it's usable in jet engines.

How every number on this page is calculated

Two separate calculations run everything below. Both are stated in full here, so any number on this page can be checked or reproduced — and so it's clear why this model's results can differ from other reported figures elsewhere.

Calculation 1

How much SAF Taiwan could produce

UCO redirected × HEFA yield (72.5%)
+ existing capacity (10,500 MT)
= Total domestic SAF

That total is then divided by Taiwan's jet fuel demand (for "% of demand") or by the 2030 target of 122,000 MT (for "% of target").

Calculation 2

How much carbon that avoids

(89 − 13.9) gCO2e/MJ × 43,000 MJ/ton
= 3.229 tCO2e avoided per ton of SAF

89 gCO2e/MJ is ICAO's baseline for conventional jet fuel; 13.9 is ICAO's exact published value for UCO-HEFA specifically — both from the same CORSIA reference table, so they're directly comparable.

Why a number here might not match another source, and why that's not an error. Different reports use different assumptions for the same real-world thing — a different HEFA yield %, a different emissions baseline, a different scope (imported SAF counted or not, one year vs. a projection). This page states its exact formulas and inputs everywhere specifically so a mismatch can be explained rather than just noticed.

Looking ahead

The 2025–2030 gap

Taiwan's committed SAF capacity is real but small, and no capacity has been announced beyond 2027. The 2030 target line shows how far that falls short if nothing else changes.

Solid line = announced, funded capacity (Formosa + CPC). It stops in 2027 because no further capacity has been publicly committed past that point — this isn't a forecast, it's the edge of what's currently known.

Where the carbon actually goes

SAF's remaining footprint, by stage

SAF isn't zero-carbon to make. Breaking its footprint into stages shows where the remaining impact actually comes from — illustrative split based on independent LCA literature, not a Taiwan-specific measurement.

Fuel production — specifically hydrogen use in hydroprocessing — is about 90% of the remaining footprint. Collection and distribution together are only about 10%. The biggest lever left to make Taiwan's SAF even cleaner isn't the cooking oil at all; it's how the hydrogen is made.

What would have to change

Four real levers

Feedstock policy

Stop exporting raw UCO

Redirecting the ~25,000 MT/yr currently shipped abroad raw could add roughly 18,000 MT of domestic SAF — more than doubling near-term capacity, at zero new infrastructure cost.

Infrastructure

Expand refining capacity past 2027

Redirecting 100% of UCO implies enough feedstock for ~72,000 MT of SAF — but known refining capacity tops out around 20,000 MT (Formosa + CPC combined). Even with unlimited feedstock, there's nowhere to process the rest. Feedstock isn't the only bottleneck.

Alternative pathway

Alcohol-to-Jet (ATJ)

Taiwan is studying ATJ production using imported ethanol as feedstock — a way to close the gap UCO alone can't, since it doesn't compete with Taiwan's limited waste-oil supply.

Regional strategy

Become a SAF import/production hub

Taiwan is exploring becoming a regional SAF hub. In the near term, this likely means continuing to import SAF to cover the gap while domestic capacity grows.

Global context

Other countries have already made this choice

Taiwan isn't the only place facing this exact tension — and it's one of the only ones that hasn't restricted UCO exports yet.

Not hypothetical

Where this is already happening in Taiwan

Taiwan's aviation industry plays a critical role in supporting economic development, international trade, and global connectivity. In 2025, China Airlines, EVA Air, and Starlux Airlines officially began using SAF at the major airports of Songshan, Taoyuan, and Kaohsiung.

Source: Taiwan's Civil Aviation Administration, reported via Hydrocarbon Processing, Apr 2025. The same report notes the 2025 SAF volume: Formosa Petrochemical's 5,500 MT plus CPC's 400 MT of imports (5,900 MT total).

Research project by Andrea Chen, who is passionate about the intersection of data science and sustainability, especially SAF. After a year of reviewing sustainable aviation fuel policy across different countries, she built this project to test how those numbers actually play out in her own country, Taiwan. She hopes this model can serve as a starting point for a more data-driven conversation about Taiwan's path to energy independence — and invites anyone working in this space, or simply curious about it, to explore the model above, question its assumptions, and get in touch.
